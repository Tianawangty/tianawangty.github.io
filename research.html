Research

The price on a grocery shelf is the end of a story that starts much earlier — in a field, at a port, inside a policy change. I follow those stories from one end to the other, using transaction-level data to see where along the way a cost gets absorbed and where it gets passed on.

Work in Progress

The Food-Price Effect of Agricultural Minimum Wage

Job market paper, with Michael Adjemian and Genti Kostandini.

Every year the U.S. Department of Labor sets a binding wage floor — the Adverse Effect Wage Rate — for the temporary agricultural workers that many U.S. farms depend on. The rate is set region by region, and a farm owes it regardless of what its own crop sells for that season. Because the increase is imposed from outside the individual farm, at a size and on a date set by federal rule, it provides a well-defined setting in which to trace a cost shock as it moves through a supply chain. This paper measures how much of it reaches the retail price, and where along the way the rest is absorbed.

The answer cannot be read off retail prices alone. The cost lands where the crop is grown; the price it might move is paid in a store hundreds of miles away. So the paper follows the cost down the supply chain one stage at a time: the farm labor market itself, using county-level agricultural employment by month and payroll by quarter; then the farm gate, using state-by-commodity monthly prices received, alongside the option to import instead, using monthly trade statistics by product code; and finally the shelf, using county-by-category retail price indices built from Nielsen retail scanner data.

Every stage is built on its corresponding treatment exposure. Each one starts from the annual change in a state's wage floor, multiplied by that state's reliance on migrant farm labor as recorded in the 2002 Census of Agriculture. Because that exposure is measured years before any of the wage changes studied, it cannot respond to them. What differs from stage to stage is the level at which the treatment is summed, and that is set by where the outcome is observed: county by county for the farm labor market, where the floor actually binds; state by commodity for farm-gate prices; and nationally, crop by crop for imports and category by category for retail, since neither an import volume nor a grocery category carries a producing state. The response is traced month by month after each announcement using local projections, across eighteen annual adjustments between 2006 and 2022. In 2025 the Department of Labor overhauled how the rate is calculated, so this work describes the regime that preceded the reform.

Presented at the AAEA Annual Meeting, Kansas City, 2026.

Food Price Inflation Across the United States

With Michael Adjemian and Qingxiao Li. Supported by USDA NIFA.

Food prices in the United States rose faster between 2020 and 2024 than at any point in four decades. That national figure is an average over local markets that moved at different times and for different reasons — some because supply was disrupted, some because demand was pushed up. This project asks how much of the rise was which, and how the answer changes from one part of the country to another. The evidence base is Circana retail scanner data covering more than 3,000 counties.

Presented at the SAEA Annual Meeting, Louisville, 2026.
