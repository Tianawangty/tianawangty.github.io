I am a Ph.D. student in Agricultural and Applied Economics at the University of Georgia. I study the economics of food in the United States — what people pay, why prices differ across regions, and how policy decisions ripple through supply chains to reshape what ends up on the table. My work draws on large-scale retail scanner data, freight records, and consumer panels to connect millions of individual transactions to the forces behind them, from labor costs in the field to how households access groceries.

The price on a grocery shelf is the end of a story that starts much earlier — in a field, at a port, inside a policy change. Across U.S. counties, the same food inflation can mask very different forces, and I use store-level scanner data to pull these apart. A rise in agricultural wages works its way through the supply chain before it reaches consumers, but how much passes through depends on whether producers can mechanize, source imports, or absorb the cost. A surge in global grain demand benefits some farming states more than others depending on how their freight corridors are built. I study these transmission paths to understand not just whether prices change, but where the pressure concentrates and who bears it.

Before my Ph.D., I spent three years as a research analyst at the International Monetary Fund, supporting country teams across southern Africa while pursuing a diverse portfolio of independent projects — from early-warning models for economic crises to climate policy evaluation — many drawing on machine learning and data science. I hold an M.S. in Data Analytics and Policy from Johns Hopkins University and a B.B.A. in Applied Economics from Hong Kong Baptist University.
